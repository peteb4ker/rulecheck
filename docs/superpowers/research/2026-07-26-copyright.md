# Benchside — Verbatim Rules Text Copyright Research

Research notes only. Not legal advice; not a substitute for review by a licensed IP attorney before shipping. Prepared per the Research Gate referenced in `docs/superpowers/specs/2026-07-26-benchside-design.md`.

## 1. The mechanics/expression line

**Statute.** 17 U.S.C. § 102(b): copyright "in no case" extends to "any idea, procedure, process, system, method of operation, concept, principle, or discovery, regardless of the form in which it is described, explained, illustrated, or embodied." This is the codification of the idea/expression dichotomy from *Baker v. Selden*, 101 U.S. 99 (1879) — Selden's bookkeeping *system* (the accounting method) was uncopyrightable even though his book explaining it was; forms necessary to use the system merged with the underlying idea.

**Merger doctrine.** *Morrissey v. Procter & Gamble Co.*, 379 F.2d 675 (1st Cir. 1967): a sweepstakes contest's rules were held uncopyrightable because there were only a limited number of ways to express those particular rules — expression "merged" with the idea, so protecting the wording would effectively grant a monopoly over the underlying idea. Morrissey is the seminal broad-merger case and is the one most directly on point for "the rules of a system, stated plainly, may have literally no protectable expression left once you subtract the idea."

**Scenes à faire.** A related judge-made gloss on § 102(b): stock elements/expressions that necessarily follow from an idea, genre, or functional requirement aren't protectable, because everyone writing in that space converges on the same expression.

**Game rules specifically — the controlling thread:**

- *Affiliated Hospital Products, Inc. v. Merdel Game Mfg. Co.*, 513 F.2d 1183 (2d Cir. 1975) (Carrom/Kik-It game rulebooks). Holding: "The rules of the game are in the public domain," and a rulebook's copyright protects only the *arrangement* and *manner of presentation* of the rules, not their content/substance. Defendant won because it did not copy the rulebook's wording verbatim — it rewrote the same rules in its own words (and even improved clarity). This is the cleanest statement of the "rules = idea, phrasing = expression" split for tabletop games, and it explicitly signals that verbatim copying of the wording would have come out differently.

- *Allen v. Academic Games League of America, Inc.*, 831 F. Supp. 785 (C.D. Cal. 1993), aff'd, 89 F.3d 614 (9th Cir. 1996). Court held plaintiff hadn't shown the expression of his game rules could be distinguished from the underlying idea/procedure of the rules — no protection for the "premises or ideas" of the games, only (potentially) for the specific physical/verbal form. Reinforces that game *procedures* (how to play, turn order, win conditions) sit outside copyright regardless of how they're described, while the specific prose describing them can carry a thin layer of protection.

- *DaVinci Editrice S.r.l. v. Ziko Games, LLC*, 183 F. Supp. 3d 820 (S.D. Tex. 2016) (Bang! vs. Legends of the Three Kingdoms) — decided at the district court, not appealed further per available records. The parties stipulated the two games have "nearly identical rules"; defendant only changed theme/artwork/prose. Court held a character's special ability is "neither literary nor artistic" — it's a functional rule/system element, not protectable expression, even though it's described in words. Notable because the *actual written wording differed* between the two games (LOTK didn't copy Bang!'s sentences), yet the court's reasoning goes further than Affiliated Hospital Products: it treats the *mechanical content* of a rule as unprotectable regardless of phrasing, which is the strongest game-rules-specific authority for "the mechanics are always free, no matter how you say it."

- *Tetris Holding, LLC v. Xio Interactive, Inc.*, 863 F. Supp. 2d 394 (D.N.J. 2012). Different fact pattern (video game look-and-feel/audiovisual expression, not written rules text), but doctrinally important as the boundary marker on the other side: the court rejected the defendant's argument that copying was automatically excused because it only copied "game rules and functions." Held that *how* a rule or function is expressed (visual design, sequencing, choices among functionally equivalent implementations) can still be protectable, because Tetris's rules could have been implemented/expressed many different ways without duplicating Tetris's specific expressive choices. Xio lost because it copied Tetris's specific expressive execution, not merely its abstract rule of "clear lines by rotating falling tetrominoes."

- *Bikram's Yoga College of India, L.P. v. Evolation Yoga, LLC*, 803 F.3d 1032 (9th Cir. 2015) — outside games but frequently cited in the same breath. A sequence of yoga poses/breathing exercises was held to be an uncopyrightable "system" or "process" under § 102(b) despite being described in a copyrighted book; only the book's specific words/pictures were protectable, not the sequence itself. Useful analogy: a rulebook's *procedure* ("draw a card, then check for special conditions, then...") is a process; only the specific sentences describing it are "the work."

**Synthesis of the line:** Courts consistently place (1) the rules/procedures/game-mechanics themselves — how many cards to draw, what a status condition does, turn structure, win conditions, penalty tiers — outside copyright entirely, as ideas/systems/processes under § 102(b), following *Baker*, *Morrissey*, *Affiliated Hospital Products*, *Allen*, and *DaVinci*. They place (2) the *specific sentences* chosen to explain those rules on the protectable side, but only thinly — merger and scenes à faire strip out any phrasing that's the only practical way to state a given rule (a real risk for terse rules-text sentences, which often *are* close to the only natural phrasing). *Tetris* is the reminder that "it's just a rule" is not a magic shield once the specific expressive execution is what's being copied wholesale rather than independently re-derived.

## 2. Verbatim reproduction risk specifically

This is the crux for Benchside: paraphrasing the *rules* is very safe (rules aren't protectable at all per §1 above); the exposure is in taking TPCi's *prose* — the actual sentences of the Rulebook, the Tournament Rules Handbook, and the Penalty Guidelines — word for word.

- **Wholesale/verbatim copying is the single fact pattern every court treats as the strongest evidence of infringement**, precisely because it forecloses the merger/idea-only defense on close inspection — you're no longer relying on "I only used the unprotectable rule," you're reproducing the author's actual expressive sentence structure, examples, transitions, and organization choices, which is exactly what *Affiliated Hospital Products* flagged as the losing move (verbatim copying), contrasted with the winning move (rewriting).
- **Fair use, 17 U.S.C. § 107, four factors, applied to Benchside's "ship documents verbatim" scenario:**
  1. *Purpose and character of use* — a free, offline lookup app is non-commercial-ish (no ads, no paywall, no data collection per the CLAUDE.md non-negotiables) but is **not transformative** if it just reproduces the text with search/navigation on top; courts have repeatedly held that adding search, indexing, or a UI wrapper around a work does not itself transform the *expressive* content (transformation requires a new purpose/meaning for the *content*, not just a new access method). This factor is close to neutral-to-negative for full verbatim reproduction; would be more favorable for short-excerpt-plus-commentary or paraphrase (added explanatory value, cross-references, judge annotations = closer to transformative).
  2. *Nature of the copyrighted work* — favorable to Benchside: rulebooks are highly factual/functional works (thin copyright to begin with, per *Affiliated Hospital Products*/*Allen*/*DaVinci*), which cuts toward fair use and also independently narrows what's even protectable.
  3. *Amount and substantiality* — for gradation (a), full verbatim text, this factor is maximally unfavorable: you're taking 100% of the protectable expression, not just the "heart" of the work but literally the whole document. Courts do allow full-work copying to be fair use when the purpose is sufficiently transformative (e.g., search-indexing cases, thumbnail cases), but a straight offline reader is a weak fit for that line of cases, which mostly involve *format-shifting for search/discovery* (find-then-buy) rather than *substituting for reading the original*.
  4. *Market effect* — mixed. TPCi's documents are already free, so there's no displaced *sale*. But market harm doctrine also covers harm to a *potential licensing market* and to the *rights holder's ability to control accuracy/currency/branding* of its rules — and courts weigh the effect if the use "were to become widespread" (i.e., if every rules app did this). TPCi could argue harm to its ability to control which version of the rules is being consulted (rules change; an offline SQLite snapshot going stale is a real and TPCi-relevant harm argument) and to a documented interest in owning the canonical "how do I look up a ruling" surface (their now-discontinued official apps, e.g., Pokémon TCG Card Dex, occupied exactly this space).
  - **Net read**: fair use is a real but not confident defense for gradation (a) full verbatim reproduction of three entire documents into a shipped product; it's a much stronger defense for (b) short verbatim excerpts embedded in an otherwise original navigational/organizational structure, and essentially a non-issue for (c) faithful paraphrase (no substantial similarity of expression = no infringement claim to begin with, no need to reach fair use).

## 3. Practical gradations, and what analogous projects do

**(a) Full verbatim text of all three documents, reproduced wholesale in-app.** Highest legal exposure. Two independent problems, not one:
  - *Copyright*: weakest fair-use position (see §2); the only saving grace is that rules documents are fact-heavy/thin-copyright works, but "we copied the whole document" is the fact pattern that has repeatedly lost even for free/nonprofit users when the copying isn't itself transformative of the expression.
  - *Contract/ToS, independent of copyright*: pokemon.com's Terms of Use (fetched July 2026) state content is licensed "for personal, noncommercial home use only," prohibit "download[ing] quantities of content to a database for any reason," and prohibit "modify[ing], or creat[ing] derivative works based on, the content." **Benchside's entire pipeline — scraping/parsing the PDFs into `content/*.json` and building `benchside.db`, a SQLite database, for redistribution inside a shipped app — is close to a textbook violation of that "no downloading quantities of content to a database" clause**, independent of whether the copyright fair-use analysis comes out favorably. This is a contract claim (breach of ToS / clickwrap, or at minimum an unlicensed-use argument bolstering a copyright claim's willfulness), not preempted by weak copyright protection in the underlying facts. This is arguably the single most concrete legal exposure identified in this research — more certain than the copyright analysis, because it doesn't depend on how a court reads *Morrissey*/*Affiliated Hospital Products*, it depends on the plain text of a contract TPCi can point to.

**(b) Verbatim short excerpts (a rule, a penalty tier, a definition) with original navigation, search, cross-referencing, categorization layered on top.** Meaningfully lower exposure on both fronts:
  - *Copyright*: short excerpts strengthen the fair-use "amount and substantiality" factor considerably; adding original structure (search index, categorization, judge-workflow-specific cross-referencing) strengthens "purpose and character" toward transformative-ish. Still verbatim, so not risk-free, but categorically different from (a).
  - *ToS*: still implicates "download quantities of content to a database" if the ingestion pipeline is copying substantial verbatim spans systematically, so this gradation reduces but does not eliminate the contract exposure — the "quantities" and "database" language doesn't obviously care about excerpt length once you've stored much of the source text as structured verbatim rows.

**(c) Faithful paraphrase — rules restated in Benchside's own words, penalty tiers and definitions rewritten while preserving the substantive information.** Lowest exposure on both fronts:
  - *Copyright*: per §1, the *rules themselves* (facts/procedures) aren't protectable at all; a faithful paraphrase that doesn't track TPCi's specific sentence structure, examples, or ordering choices closely should fall outside "substantial similarity of protectable expression" entirely — this is exactly the *Affiliated Hospital Products* winning fact pattern (Merdel rewrote Carrom's rules and won on the merits, not fair use).
  - *ToS*: paraphrase is not "the content" being downloaded to a database — it's an independent expression of the same facts, which is what copyright law says everyone is entitled to do. This gradation is the only one that credibly evades the ToS's anti-derivative-works clause too, since a paraphrase isn't "based on" the content's protected expression, just the same underlying facts.

**Analogous projects — MTG comparison is weaker than initially assumed.** Wizards of the Coast's Fan Content Policy (fetched July 2026, company.wizards.com/en/legal/fancontentpolicy) explicitly states Fan Content **does not include** "the verbatim copying and reposting of Wizards' IP (e.g., freely distributing D&D® rules content or books...)," **even for free distribution**. So the premise in the task brief — "MTG's Comprehensive Rules are reproduced verbatim by many third-party sites/apps under WotC's Fan Content Policy" — does not hold up under the policy's own text; verbatim reproduction is the one thing the policy carves *out*, not what it licenses. What actually makes MTG rules apps (e.g., the "MTG Rules" Android app, Yawgatog's hyperlinked rules) low-risk in practice is a different fact: **Wizards itself publishes the Comprehensive Rules as a free, unrestricted-by-a-clickwrap-paywall TXT/PDF/DOCX download** (media.wizards.com/.../MagicCompRules_*.txt) specifically because the rules text is meant to be *the* reference other tools build on — this is closer to an implied license through open publication practice than an express Fan Content Policy grant, and it is a **materially different posture than TPCi's**, whose Terms of Use affirmatively prohibit database-scale downloading and derivative works of its site content.

**TPCi analogous policy check.** No TPCi/Pokémon-specific "fan content policy" comparable to WotC's was found. Pokemon.com's general Terms of Use (§ above) are the only governing document located, and they are more restrictive than WotC's, not more permissive — no carve-out for rules-reference tools was found. TPCi has historically run its own official rules-lookup products (the "Pokémon TCG Card Dex" app, discontinued per support.pokemon.com, and the sunset Pokémon TCG Online client), which signals TPCi treats "official rules/card lookup" as a product category it wants to own, not one it has signaled openness to third parties occupying — the opposite of Wizards' posture of freely publishing raw rules files for the ecosystem to build on.

## 4. Bottom line — risk assessment per gradation

| | Copyright exposure | ToS/contract exposure | TPCi enforcement likelihood | Recommendation |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **(a) Full verbatim, all 3 docs** | Real, not frivolous — thin-copyright factual work + weak transformativeness argument for a straight offline reader; strongest fact pattern for a plaintiff | High — squarely implicates ToS's "no database-scale downloading" and "no derivative works" clauses; contract claim doesn't depend on copyright's thin-protection wrinkle | Low-to-moderate for a small free indie app with no press/revenue (per former TPCi Chief Legal Officer Don McGowan's public comments: enforcement is triggered mainly by *profit* and *press attention*, C&Ds are reactive not proactive-hunting), but **not zero**, and severity if it happens is high (an App Store DMCA takedown kills distribution instantly; TPCi's history includes a $15M judgment and active suits, e.g., v. Palworld's Pocketpair in 2024, showing willingness to litigate hard when it chooses to) | **Do not ship this gradation.** The clean contract-language violation makes this the one gradation where "the rules aren't copyrightable" doesn't save you — the exposure isn't really a copyright-merger question at all. |
| **(b) Short verbatim excerpts + original structure** | Moderate-low — meaningfully better fair-use posture (amount factor, some transformativeness from added navigation/cross-referencing), but still real if the pipeline is systematically capturing large verbatim spans across three documents | Moderate — better than (a) but the ToS's "quantities...to a database" language isn't excerpt-length-limited; a database of thousands of verbatim rule-text rows is still arguably "quantities...to a database" | Similar profile to (a): low baseline enforcement probability for a small free app, same tail risk | **Workable only for genuinely short, individually-justified quotes** (e.g., quoting a specific penalty-tier label or a card-effect ruling verbatim where paraphrase would itself risk changing meaning), not as the default content strategy. Treat as an exception path, not the plan. |
| **(c) Faithful paraphrase** | Low — rules/procedures are uncopyrightable facts; a genuine paraphrase (different sentence structure, own examples/organization) is the fact pattern that has *won* on the merits (*Affiliated Hospital Products*), not just survived on fair use | Low — paraphrase isn't "the content," so the ToS's derivative-works and database clauses have a much weaker textual hook | Low, and even if TPCi objected, the legal footing to actually stop a good-faith paraphrase is weak on both copyright and contract theories | **This is the only gradation consistent with CLAUDE.md's own non-negotiable that verbatim text is "gated on the Research Gate"** — recommend building the shipping product on faithful paraphrase, reserving verbatim quotation (gradation b) for narrow, individually-reviewed cases, and treating gradation (a) as off the table. |

**One-line bottom line:** the *rules* are free to use no matter what (idea/process doctrine is settled and consistent across every game-rules case found); the *risk is entirely in the prose*, and it's compounded — not just mitigated — by the fact that TPCi's own Terms of Use separately and explicitly forbid exactly what a "scrape 3 PDFs into a shipped SQLite database" pipeline does, which is a contract problem that exists independently of how favorably the copyright fair-use analysis might otherwise read.
